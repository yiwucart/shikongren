> **Translator's note.** Sky burial and water burial leave no ancestral grave, and critics of this school have used that fact as a refutation: if there is no grave, what is there to read? The original answers with some heat — much of it directed at fellow practitioners by name-calling, which has been reduced here to the argument itself. The substance is a substitution: when there is no grave, the dwelling of the pregnancy takes its place. Anyone who has read the earlier essay on pregnancy-period feng shui will recognise the mechanism.

The challenge is usually put as a trap. Tibetan families practise sky burial, some coastal communities practise water burial, and neither leaves a marked grave that descendants can tend. If feng shui is read from graves, the argument runs, then these people must be outside its reach — and a theory that cannot read them is a theory with a hole in it.

The challenge fails, and it fails for a reason that is visible in the question itself.

## The question answers itself

Sky burial and water burial are not exotic variants of grave feng shui. They are the **absence** of a grave.

So the question "how do you read feng shui for a sky burial" reduces to "how do you read feng shui for a family with no ancestral grave." That is not a special case. It is the ordinary condition of a great many people — migrants, the dispersed, families whose graves were destroyed or whose burial records were lost, and anyone in a society where cremation and scattering are normal.

A reader who cannot answer for a family without a grave cannot answer for a large part of the modern world. That is the real gap, and it is not created by Tibetan practice.

## What replaces the grave

The substitution is stated flatly: for a person with no ancestral grave, read **the dwelling the mother occupied during most of the pregnancy**.

This is not a fallback invented for the occasion. The school holds that the pregnancy dwelling and the ancestral grave act on the same thing — the level of a person's fate — and act with comparable force. That is why the earlier essay in this archive could compare them directly, and why a family with a strong grave but a poor pregnancy dwelling does not get the result the grave alone would predict.

The reasoning is about exposure. A person's formation happens somewhere. For nine months before birth, that somewhere is one building, occupied continuously, through the whole of the period in which the body and its constitution are being assembled. If a site's configuration acts on the people who live on it, then the site a person lived on before being born is the site with the longest uninterrupted contact of their life.

For a family with a grave, the two sources combine, and the grave is usually the stronger of the two because it acts across generations rather than across months. For a family without a grave, one source remains, and it is sufficient to make a reading.

## Why siblings born in the same house differ

The obvious objection comes next, and it is a fair one: if siblings were all born in the same house, and that house is the substitute for the grave, why do they have different fates?

The answer is the same mechanism that governs graves, applied one level down.

Siblings share a dwelling, but they do not share a **positional point**. Under the assignment rules, each child is fixed to a different layer of the site's layout according to birth order. Different layers correspond to different sectors. Different sectors contain different sand and water.

So the house is the same and the reading is not. One child's point falls in a sector with good sand; another's falls in a sector with harmful water; a third's falls in one of the nine sectors that want nothing. Same building, three different verdicts, because the building is not being read as a whole — it is being read at three points.

This is exactly why the crude version of feng shui, which assigns a verdict to a house and then applies it to everyone inside, produces so many failures. A house is not auspicious or inauspicious in general. It is auspicious for the people positioned on its good sectors and inauspicious for the people positioned on its bad ones.

## Why a shared site runs down over generations

One more objection, and it is the sharpest: if the pregnancy dwelling substitutes for a grave, why can several generations not all be read from the same house?

Because the energy in a site is finite.

The comparison the school uses is a vessel. A container of a fixed size holds a fixed amount of water, and a fixed amount of rice. If more people eat from it, each gets less. The vessel does not refill because the queue is longer.

A site — auspicious or inauspicious — holds a fixed quantity of energy. As the number of people depending on it increases, the share available to each declines. The tenth generation living in the same house is not receiving what the first generation received. It is receiving a tenth of it, or less.

This is why the same house can produce a distinguished line for two generations and then produce nothing remarkable for five. The site did not change. The division did.

It also explains why a grave can serve a large family without visible dilution while a dwelling cannot: the grave's energy is tapped across a wider span and at a different level, and the assignment rules route different descendants to different layers rather than piling them all on one point. A dwelling has no such depth. Everyone who lives in it is living in the same small volume.

## What this establishes

Three things, in ascending order of usefulness.

A family without a grave is not outside the system. It has one fewer input, not zero inputs.

The pregnancy dwelling is a real reading, not a consolation prize. For a great many people alive now — the cremated, the scattered, the migrated, the descendants of the uprooted — it is the only reading available, and it is enough to establish a level.

And the reason siblings differ is not mystery. It is position. The same reason applies whether the site is a grave or a house, which is the point: the method does not have a special case for Tibet. It has one method, and it reads whatever site a person was actually exposed to.

For a reader in a country where almost nobody has an ancestral grave, that is not a marginal application of the tradition. It is the main one.
