> **Translator's note.** Companion piece to the previous one, and a sharper case. “Dian xue” (点穴) normally means striking a pressure point on the body; here it means putting an object in your own courtyard to “paralyse” a mountain that is troubling you. The objection is not that the object is the wrong object. It is that the target is in the wrong place, and that a small thing cannot restrain a large one — the same argument, applied where it is hardest to answer.

---


A practitioner looks at the hill across the valley, tells you it is harming your household, and offers a solution. You put something in your own courtyard — the details vary, and imagination is permitted — and by doing so you “pin” the mountain. Pinned, it can no longer affect you.

## Where is the point being struck?

Start with the geography. A pressure point is a place on a body. If the mountain is the body, then the point to be struck is on the mountain.

So why is the object in your courtyard?

Put the question concretely: who hangs a mirror on the neighbour's house to fix their own feng shui? Nobody, because nobody would be allowed. The neighbour would take it down, and the neighbour would be right to. Yet the same practitioner will explain, without embarrassment, that pinning the mountain from your own garden works — that by acting on your own property you have restrained an object on someone else's.

That is not a technique. It is a category error with a price attached.

## Even granting the premise

Suppose we grant it. Suppose the practitioner can locate the mountain's vital point — a claim worth examining in itself — and suppose his object is placed precisely there. What is the object?

A pillar. A figurine. A needle of some kind. Anything the customer will buy.

The mountain weighs what a mountain weighs. The question is the same one the mirror raises: where does the force come from? If a single column could hold a mountain in place, engineering would be a much simpler profession than it is. Nothing in the procedure addresses magnitude. It addresses only the customer's willingness to believe that a small object aimed at a large one constitutes control.

## The purpose of a dragon

There is a deeper problem, and it is not physical.

The point of working with a dragon vein — the ridge line said to carry the earth's energy — is to **use** it. You want its energy to arrive at your site. You want the vein's accumulated qi to flow into your grave or your house.

Now consider what pinning does. It stops the thing from moving. A dragon that cannot move delivers nothing. The old stories even supply the object for it: the sea-calming needle, the pillar that fixes the sea in place. If you drove that into your dragon, you would have succeeded in killing the only asset you had. Why would anyone want this?

The image is worth keeping because it shows what the technique actually is. It is a story about domination, dressed as a story about benefit. The two do not fit together, and the practitioner never has to notice, because the customer does not ask what happens after the mountain stops moving.

## Which point, and who decides?

Underneath all of this sits an unanswerable question: where is the point?

The practitioner says it is where he says it is. Press him. What theory establishes that this spot and not the one three metres away is the mountain's vital point? What measurement distinguishes them? What observation would show that he was wrong?

There is no such observation, which is exactly why the claim can be made confidently. A statement that cannot fail is cheap to produce and impossible to refute, and in a market where the client cannot check, cheap and unfalsifiable wins.

## The pressure-point concession

None of this denies that pressing points on a human body can do something. It can. Certain points, pressed correctly, relieve particular complaints. That is a real, limited technique with a real, limited range.

But notice what it cannot do. If pressure points decided fights, the champions of full-contact competition would all come from the tradition that has studied them most carefully for the longest time. They do not. A trained fighter standing in front of you, free to move, is not going to be stopped by a thumb.

And a bullet does not look for a point at all. It goes through the chest and the outcome is decided by where it went and how much energy it carried. **Energy decides who lives and who dies.** Not the location of a point.

The same holds for buildings. Pinning, deflection, the four ounces and the thousand pounds — all of these may be well-intentioned, and none of them works unless the intervention reaches the required magnitude. Below that threshold the entire vocabulary is decorative. Above it, you no longer need the vocabulary, because you are doing something that can be measured.
