> **Translator's note.** The companion to the previous piece, and the more polemical of the two. It takes the most familiar object in Chinese exam folklore — the Wen Chang pagoda, sold in every stationery shop and every temple forecourt — and argues that its position is wrong, its material matters more than its position, and that in most cases it does nothing at all. The middle section, on how much of an essay mark depends on which teacher opens the envelope, is the most uncomfortable and most convincing passage in the archive. It closes with an argument against traditional day-selection that turns on a pair of spring couplets in one southern Chinese city.

Every year, before the university entrance examination, families buy a Wen Chang pagoda — a small nine-storey tower, usually brass or blackwood — and place it in the south-east corner of a room. The tradition says the south-east is the seat of learning, and the pagoda is the god of literature's own furniture.

The objection is not that the object is powerless. It is that **the south-east is the wrong socket** — and in most houses, for most children, nothing is plugged into it.

## Plug and socket

Think of the child's chart as a plug. Each stem and branch in it is a pin. Think of the compass as a wall of sockets: twenty-four of them, each wired to a different thing.

A pagoda in the south-east places a load on the 辰, 巽 and 巳 sockets. Whether anything happens depends entirely on whether that socket is wired to anything in this particular plug. For a child whose chart has no live connection there, the tower is a lamp in a socket that goes nowhere. **Nothing is broken. Nothing is powered.**

The author's example child needs 甲 and 子. A pagoda in 甲 works a thousand times better than the same pagoda in the south-east — same tower, same brass, different socket. And the general form of the error is what he objects to: the classical method prescribes one position for everyone, which is to say it does not consider the person at all, and therefore cannot be right about any particular person.

## The material matters more than the position

Even granting the right corner, there is a second variable almost nobody considers: **what the pagoda is made of.**

Wood, metal, fire, earth, water — the same tower in five materials, in the same corner, produces five different results. In the author's account of this particular child:

- A **metal** pagoda causes a blunder on the day. Not a small one: the paper that would have been written well is written badly. This is the trap — the family buys the handsome brass one because it looks like the ones in the photographs.
- A **black wood** or ebony pagoda helps. Questions the child could not answer, guessed at as the papers are collected, come out right.
- A **red wood** pagoda helps differently: the paper is finished early and without obstruction.

Note what this implies. The remedy is not a symbol with a general meaning. It is an object with a specific composition, placed in a specific relation, at a specific time — and the composition can invert the effect. **A wrongly made remedy is not a weak remedy; it is the opposite remedy.**

There is a further case, in the 子 direction: a pagoda of stone produces a marking error. The essay is good, the argument is sound, and it is marked down because the examiner disagrees with it.

## The examiner

Which brings up the part of the examination that nobody wants to discuss.

An essay is not marked by a rule; it is marked by a person, and a person marking their two-hundredth script is not the same person who marked the first. Early in a batch there is patience and a rubric. Later there is fatigue, impression, and the examiner's own opinions doing the work.

The author's example is deliberately provocative. Set the essay question: *What do you think of the Yijing?* A script that is clumsy and badly written but says the Yijing is a science gets a high mark. A graceful script that calls it superstition gets a low one. **The mark was decided before the essay was read** — decided by which examiner opened the envelope.

In language subjects, therefore, there is an irreducible risk that has nothing to do with the candidate's ability: the distance between what the candidate thinks and what the marker thinks. And if that is true, then the hours around the examination — what the candidate wears, what they eat, who drives them — are not decoration. They are part of the configuration that decides which pile the script lands in. And the configuration does not end at the classroom door: it runs through the marking period, until the result is published.

Whether or not one accepts the mechanism, the observation about examiners is one that anyone who has marked a thousand essays will recognise. The method's claim is only that the mechanism is physical rather than psychological — that the paper is *carried* to the sympathetic examiner rather than *judged* favourably by chance.

## Two different jobs

A question that comes up constantly: when should the adjustment be made?

Study and examination are two different problems with two different time constants.

**Study is measured in years.** It is a standing condition. Some charts need one adjustment for life; some need one every few years; some need one every year. Yearly is the most effective.

**An examination is measured in days.** Once the date is fixed, the analysis moves from the year to the month, day and hour — six pillars become eight or nine, and the method becomes harder without changing in kind.

Which means the adjustment for a specific sitting can be very short and very specific. Suppose the examination falls on a 庚子 day. Where should the candidate sleep the night before, and which direction should they travel to the hall? A candidate living in the 癸 direction who sits in the 丁 direction has the best available arrangement, and can book a hotel on the 癸 side of the venue for the preceding nights. In those last days, the food and the conduct are narrowed to the day.

The logic is the same. Only the resolution changes.

## Day-selection is folklore

The last question is whether the adjustment itself needs an auspicious date. No: any time will do. Which opens onto a broader argument, because day-selection is the most commercially successful practice in the whole tradition and the author regards it as empty.

Traditional day-selection, on this account, has **no predictive value at all** and survives as custom. The test is simple and cannot be answered: take an auspicious day and go to a hospital. Take an inauspicious day and go to the same hospital. On both days, at both hours, people die, people are revived, children are born. So which is the auspicious hour?

The illustration he prefers is a pair of spring couplets.

### The white-edged couplets of Zhangzhou

In one city in Fujian — Zhangzhou — the red couplets pasted on doors at New Year have **white borders at top and bottom.** Nowhere else in China does this. To an outsider, and by the ordinary rules of the custom, a white border on a New Year couplet is a bad omen: white is the colour of mourning.

The origin is historical. In the Ming dynasty, in one year, the able-bodied men of the district were killed repelling a raid from the sea. The red couplets had already been pasted on every door. To take them down would have been worse than to leave them. So the survivors pasted white strips along the top and bottom edges — mourning added to the celebration already in place — and the practice held.

Centuries later, Zhangzhou still does it. **And Zhangzhou prospers, is promoted, and fills its houses with grandchildren at exactly the same rate as the districts that paste plain red.** Whatever the white border means, it does not mean misfortune.

The conclusion is not that symbols are meaningless. It is that **custom and efficacy are two different things**, and a practice can be universally observed, deeply felt, and completely inert. Which is the argument against reading the calendar for a good day, and for treating the whole question as one of fit rather than fortune.

## One last thing, about method

Underneath the pagodas and the dates is a claim about what the accessible part of a fate is.

**It is physical, not psychological.** The remedy is a direction, a material, a food, a person — not a change of attitude, not a reframing, not a conversation about how you feel about your future. The author is explicit that the psychological reading of these practices is the wrong one: the objects are not placebos that work by reassuring the family, and if they worked that way, one pagoda would do as well as another.

That is the position, stated as plainly as the archive states it anywhere. It is also the position that makes the whole system vulnerable — because it can be checked, and it will be wrong sometimes.

For the particular child in these two pieces, the last prescription is the one he most needs. His problem is not intelligence but restlessness: the peer star is stirred, and a stirred peer star cannot sit still. He reads for a while and gets up. **Releasing hares is recommended** — not for the hares, but because the element that keeps him in his chair is the one the hares are made of, and the act of release is the one that brings it in.
