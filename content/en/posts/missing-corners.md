> **Translator's note.** "Missing corner" (*quējiǎo*, 缺角) is one of the most repeated claims in popular feng shui: a floor plan that is not a perfect rectangle, and some sector of life is said to be damaged. This essay argues that the claim is not merely inaccurate but commercially manufactured — and the last section shows exactly how a practitioner can make it appear to work on any home, every time.

---


A flat with a corner cut off is supposed to damage one part of your life, and a flat without one is supposed to protect it. The claim is repeated so often that it is treated as elementary. It is not elementary, and it is not true. What follows is six arguments, in the order the original gives them: what the record shows, what a modern city actually contains, why the claim is made, what a missing corner can and cannot do, why it is not the deciding factor, and finally how the reading is made to succeed.

## 1. What the record shows

Look at the facts first. In the old society, only the rich had houses without missing corners; the houses of the poor nearly all had them. If missing corners were inauspicious, that pattern should mean the rich flourished and the poor did not.

It does not mean that. Wealthy households died out more often than poor ones. Large landholding families frequently ended without heirs, while poor households had child after child. There is an old saying that the poor man has many sons, and it is an observation, not a joke.

The same pattern holds in an industrial society. Birth rates among manual workers and farmers are not lower than among the comfortable urban professional class — they are markedly higher. The better the material conditions, the fewer the children; the harder the conditions, the more. This is not a matter of chance. Every organism under existential threat reproduces harder. Laboratory animals under lethal pressure breed at higher rates, not lower. During major wars, human fertility tends to rise rather than fall: the faster the dying, the faster the births.

And the dynastic founders are the counter-example that settles the argument. The Zhu family house that produced the founding emperor of the Ming had missing corners. So did the Zhao family house that produced the founding emperor of the Song. Your own family house had no missing corners — your grandfather was a landlord, and here you are reading fortunes. A missing corner and the fortune of a house are simply not the same variable. That is the factual record.

## 2. In a modern city, every flat has them

Now suppose a house has ten thousand missing corners. Congratulations — I would trade a house with none for it, and I will tell you why.

Ten thousand missing corners means at least eight hundred or a thousand separate flats. And in a contemporary city, is there any flat without one? The moment a building has a lift shaft or a stair core, the floor plate is no longer a rectangle. Essentially one hundred per cent of urban flats have missing corners. Many rural houses do not.

So compare the two populations. Are the urban people in their corner-cut flats less happy, less prosperous, less healthy than the rural people in their rectangular ones? Look at the visible facts rather than the theory. The more missing corners a household owns, the wealthier it tends to be — because owning many flats is what wealth looks like. The fewer, the less. This needs no reasoning at all. As I have said elsewhere: when logic and science cannot settle a question, look at the facts.

## 3. Why the claim is made

Why do practitioners talk about missing corners? Because talking about missing corners generates work.

Every flat in the city has them. A claim that flags a defect present in every building in the country produces an unlimited supply of clients and an unlimited supply of remedies to sell. This is a commercial mechanism. It has nothing to do with whether the claim is true.

## 4. A missing corner is not always inauspicious

Speaking rationally, a missing corner is not uniformly bad. It is sometimes slightly good. It can be either.

The reason is straightforward: what a missing corner removes is a sector of the building, and whether that sector was carrying auspicious or inauspicious sand-water determines the sign of what was lost. Cut away a bad sector and the building is improved. The direction of the effect depends on the direction of the cut.

## 5. It is not the deciding factor

Whether a missing corner is auspicious or inauspicious, it does not determine the fortune of the house.

The fortune of a dwelling is decided by what lies outside it. If a munition works explodes next door, no amount of rectangular floor plate will help you. If the surrounding terrain and the incoming sand-water are wrong, no floor plan can correct them.

So ask the practical question: if a missing corner does not decide the outcome, why fix your attention on it? It is a secondary factor. Where is the account of the primary one?

## 6. How the reading is made to succeed every time

Here is the part that matters, and it explains why the claim survives in the market.

A missing corner can be used to hit the target every single time. Readers who have not seen the mechanism will not understand how that is possible. The answer is that any urban flat has at least a dozen missing corners, and a dozen missing corners is a dozen independent claims, each attached to a different domain of life. Watch how it is done:

- This corner is why your finances are unstable.
- That corner is why your child's marks are disappointing.
- This one means your spouse will stray.
- That one means the woman of the house falls ill.
- This one means your husband is bullied at work.
- That one means the child is sickly.
- This one points to cerebrovascular trouble in the father.
- And this one indicates that your husband has a romantic entanglement.

If she objects that her husband has no such entanglement, the answer is ready: the time has not yet come, the energy is not yet sufficient. With one flat's worth of missing corners a practitioner can cover an entire life. And if the subject is illness rather than conduct, the same method assigns this corner to the liver, that one to the lungs, this one to the stomach, that one to the limbs, this one to the head. One of them will land. The client will not suspect anything, because the claims were generated from the building rather than from the person, and the person will recognise themselves in whichever one happens to fit.

I have seen this work on people who knew better. Had I not explained the mechanism to them, they would have concluded that the reading was uncanny.

This is not prediction. It is a technique for producing the impression of prediction, and it is the reason practitioners who lead with missing corners should be treated as salesmen rather than readers.

## So does a missing corner matter at all?

It can. As said above, it can have an effect — a secondary one, often small enough to ignore. What it cannot do is carry the weight that is placed on it: it cannot decide whether a dwelling is auspicious, it cannot assign a life event to a person, and it cannot be diagnosed from a floor plan alone.

The theory of missing corners is not a fragment of feng shui that has been handed down imperfectly. It is a commercial device dressed as one.
