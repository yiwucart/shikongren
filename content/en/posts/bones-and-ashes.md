> **Translator's note.** Cremation is now the majority practice in much of Europe and North America, and the question that follows — if my ancestors were cremated, or their ashes were scattered, is there any ancestral feng shui left to read? — is one that Chinese sources almost never address, because burial was the norm. This piece addresses it directly. Its answers are more radical than most Western readers will expect, and they are arrived at from the school's own premises rather than from sentiment.

---


Two questions get run together whenever this subject comes up. The first is whether a cremated ancestor still affects descendants at all. The second is whether ashes and a buried body have the same effect.

They are separate, and the answers are different in kind. On the first, the answer is yes: both affect descendants. On the second, the answer is yes, but only slightly, and the difference is smaller than the difference between a good site and a bad one.

## Both affect descendants; bones slightly more

A buried body and a container of ashes both exercise influence on the descendants of the person they came from. Neither is inert.

Bones carry slightly more of it. The reason given is one of quantity rather than kind: the ashes are a smaller portion of what the body was, and on the school's account the influence scales with the amount of matter that remains in relation to the line. Less matter, less coupling.

That is the whole of the difference. It is real, and it is small.

## Why the quantity matters — and what follows from it

Take the principle seriously and a striking consequence follows.

If the influence runs through what remains of the body, then a family that does not want ancestral influence has a simple remedy available: disperse the ashes. No grave, no ancestral influence, and the household is subject only to its own dwelling.

This is stated as a technical observation, not as a recommendation. It follows from the mechanism, and the school states it plainly: with no ancestral grave, the inauspicious energies that a grave can transmit are no longer received. The descendants are then affected by their own dwellings and by nothing upstream of them.

It is worth flagging how radical this is in the Chinese context, where the ancestral grave is treated as a permanent obligation. The argument here is not that the obligation should be abandoned. It is that the mechanism is a mechanism, and it can be described without being recommended.

## The Tibetan case as evidence

The proof offered is ethnographic rather than experimental, and it is a good one.

Tibetan families practise sky burial, and so have no ancestral graves at all. On the ordinary account, such families should be entirely outside the reach of ancestral feng shui — no grave, no influence, nothing to read.

That is precisely what the school says, and it names what remains. For a Tibetan, only two kinds of feng shui apply: the dwelling the family occupies, and the place the mother lived during the pregnancy. Everything that a Chinese household attributes to the ancestral grave is, for a Tibetan family, simply absent.

The case does double duty. It confirms that the grave is a specific mechanism rather than a general metaphor for ancestry, and it supplies the substitute: when there is no grave, the pregnancy-period dwelling carries the weight.

## Scientifically, no grave is the better arrangement

Put the moral question aside for a moment and follow the logic.

If the grave transmits both auspicious and inauspicious energy, and if a family has no control over what a grave's site actually is, then a grave is a risk as well as an asset. Not having one removes the risk along with the asset.

The stated conclusion is that from a purely technical standpoint, no ancestral grave is the safer configuration, because the inauspicious component is no longer received at all.

This is where the tradition and the technique part company, and the essay says so. Chinese custom holds that the bones and ashes of ancestors cannot simply be discarded, that a family without ancestors is not a family. That is a moral position, and this school does not argue against it. It only notes that the position is moral rather than technical, and that the two should not be confused when a family is deciding what to do.

## Why cremation is the cleaner option

The practical recommendation is cremation, and the reasoning is not feng shui at all — it is hygiene.

Ashes are clean. They have been through temperatures that leave nothing alive in them. A container of ashes can be kept in a house without any concern of that kind; the essay states that it can be kept at home without difficulty.

Bones are a different matter. A body buried in the ordinary way, or bones recovered and brought indoors, carry a wide range of pathogenic organisms. Handling them is genuinely hazardous. The essay puts it bluntly: bringing the bones home can be dangerous to your life in a way that bringing the ashes home is not.

So the recommendation is cremation, on grounds that would be accepted by any public health authority, and the feng shui difference is small enough that it does not outweigh them.

## The conclusion that matters

The comparison between bones and ashes is being asked at the wrong scale.

One hundred per cent influence and eighty per cent influence do not turn a wealthy man into a beggar. The difference between them is a matter of degree within a range. What matters is not which material remains, but whether the influence that arrives is auspicious or inauspicious.

A well-sited grave with ashes in it will do more for a family than a badly sited grave with a complete skeleton. A family that has followed the mechanism to its logical end — no grave at all — is not in a worse position than one with a mediocre grave. It is in a different position, and on this account a cleaner one.

The question to ask is therefore not "where are the remains" but "what is the quality of the site, if there is a site." That question has an answer. The other one, in most of the cases where it gets asked, does not change anything.
