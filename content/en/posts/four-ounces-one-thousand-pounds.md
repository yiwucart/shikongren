> **Translator's note.** The idiom 四两拨千斤 — “four ounces deflect a thousand pounds” — is the standard justification for every small remedy sold to counter a large problem: a mirror against an office tower, a charm against a roof corner. The piece is a single sustained objection, and it is arithmetic rather than metaphysics. It is also the fullest statement of an argument that appears in compressed form elsewhere in this archive.

---


A practitioner stands in your doorway, points at the corner of a building opposite, and tells you it is cutting into your house. What is to be done? He has a mirror for it. Or he will draw a talisman and hang it there.

Does it work?

It does, he says. Four ounces can move a thousand pounds.

## Do the arithmetic

Let us accept the ratio for the sake of argument and see what it implies.

The building opposite weighs some tens of thousands of tonnes. If four ounces of mirror can move a thousand pounds of building, then to deflect that structure proportionally you would need a compass weighing a tonne or two — bolted to your wall, swinging on a pivot, physically pushing the tower aside. Nobody sells that. The mirror on your lintel weighs a few hundred grams.

The idiom, taken literally, does not describe a small remedy. It describes a lever of enormous mechanical advantage, and the whole point of a lever is that the load is on the other end of it. There is no lever here. There is a disc of glass.

## Where the idiom comes from, and why it does not transfer

Four ounces really can deflect a thousand pounds — in a fight. If a large man charges you, meeting him head-on is expensive; stepping to the side and redirecting his momentum costs almost nothing. That is the technique the phrase describes, and it is real. But notice the conditions: you need a body already in motion, a direction to borrow, and a point of contact.

Now put an infant in that position. Your daughter has just given birth. Tell the baby to deflect you. She has the technique available to her in principle — and she has nothing to apply it with. She does not have four ounces. Or suppose she does, and you are not a thousand pounds but ten thousand: the required force scales with the load, and four ounces becomes four pounds, which she also does not have.

The metaphor works when the small side has leverage. A mirror has none.

## One grain of rice

None of this means the mirror does nothing at all. It does something. It is simply not something that can be counted.

If you have not eaten for three days, one grain of rice has an effect. It is a real grain, and it does what a grain does. It also does not address the fact that you are starving. To describe that grain as “food” in a way that implies the hunger is handled is not a lie about the grain. It is a lie about the scale.

This is the honest status of most remedy objects: real effect, negligible magnitude. What they reliably produce is the feeling that something has been done — which is not nothing, and which is not what the client paid for.

## The injection

A mother takes her child to the clinic. The child is frightened of the needle. The mother says: don't be afraid, it won't hurt. The child stops crying. The injection is given.

Did it hurt?

Of course it hurt. The reassurance did not remove the pain; it changed the child's behaviour while the pain was happening. That is a genuine effect, and it is worth having. But if a doctor told you a treatment worked because the patient stopped crying, you would not accept it as evidence about the disease.

## Why the saying survives

The saying survives because it is load-bearing. Remove it and the product line collapses. A mirror that can deflect a building justifies its price; a mirror that provides reassurance at the level of a grain of rice does not. The phrase is not a mistake about physics. It is a necessary piece of sales language, and it has been repeated so often, by people who never did the arithmetic, that it now sounds like tradition.

It is not tradition. It is a ratio nobody checked.
