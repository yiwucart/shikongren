> **Translator's note.** A short technical piece that turns on one distinction most readings miss: zero is not water. A sea or a large lake is treated as the zero plane — an absence, not an expanse of the water element — and the rest of the site is read against it. The worked example at the end is a grave above the Fujian coast; the images in the original carry Chinese labels and have been omitted here.

---


The method for coastal and lakeside sites rests on a single definition, and if the definition is wrong everything downstream is wrong. It concerns what the sea is: not an expanse of the water element, but an absence of terrain — the zero plane against which every other height is measured.

## What counts as sea

Not every body of water. The category covers open, unbroken water — the sea, a very large lake, water with no far shore you can see. It does not cover a small bay, a reservoir, a river, a pond, or a canal.

The reason for the restriction is that the method treats this kind of water as **the zero plane**: the baseline against which every elevation in the surrounding terrain is measured. A reservoir has a far bank, and the far bank is a hill. An open sea has no far bank, so it supplies the datum.

Zero is not the water element. This is the point that gets lost. In this system, water is an element with a direction and a character; zero is a reference surface, like the sea-level line on a map. A site with sea to its east does not have water in the east. It has **nothing** in the east, and everything else is measured from that nothing.

## The three configurations

**Sea to the east, a hill to the west.** East is zero. The hill in the west is sand. That is the whole reading: one zero, one sand, the rest of the compass filled in against them.

**Sea to the east, but high buildings between you and it.** Now the sea is not your datum. If the buildings on that side are taller than your site, they block the line, and the sea has no bearing on you at all — the buildings are what you are reading. This is common in coastal cities, where a sea view sells the apartment and the tower next door determines the feng shui.

**Sea to the east, but everything between you and the water is lower than you.** Then the whole run from your site down to the shoreline is treated as zero, not just the water itself. You are standing at the top of a zero slope, and the reading is made from your elevation outwards.

## When there is water beyond the hill

A more complicated case, and the one that separates careful readings from careless ones.

East is open sea. West is a low hill. Beyond the hill, a river.

Here the hill in the west is sand, but it belongs to the **local** frame — the near field, the immediate setting of the site. The river beyond it is water, and it belongs to the **large** frame — the first ring of the wider landscape. The sea in the east remains zero throughout.

The mistake is to treat the sea and the river as two waters facing each other across the site — what this system calls paired water in opposition. They are not a pair. The sea is a datum and the river is an element, and a datum cannot be paired with anything. Read the sea as water and the entire configuration inverts: a site that should be read as receiving good water from the west is read as receiving water from both sides, and the verdict flips.

The instruction in the original is to imagine the sea surface as wind moving across a field of wheat — a plane, textured, continuous, and flat. That image keeps the category straight. Wheat is not water.

## The worked case

A successful businessman in Fujian. His grandmother's grave.

East of the grave: open sea, unbroken to the horizon. West of the grave: a gentle sand slope rising. West of that slope: a river.

Read with the sea as zero: the river in the west is the large-frame water of the site, and the slope between is the near-field sand. The man is the eldest grandson of the deceased. In the positional system used here — which assigns compass sectors to family members by generation and birth order — the eldest grandson of the main line falls in the western sector.

He is therefore positioned in the auspicious water of the large frame, which is the river. The reading and the outcome agree, and the agreement depends entirely on not counting the sea as water. Count it as water and the western river becomes half of an opposed pair, the sector loses its status, and the same grave reads as ordinary.

## The general lesson

Coastal sites are where the difference between a defined term and an undefined one becomes visible. Practitioners who treat “water” as a mood — anything wet, anywhere — produce readings that cannot be checked, because nothing in them is fixed. Fix the sea at zero and the rest of the site has to be measured. That is the whole benefit of the definition: it removes the freedom to call the result either way.
